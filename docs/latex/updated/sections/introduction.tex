\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Temporal prediction differs from ordinary supervised learning because a useful
representation must retain information from a potentially unbounded past.
Reservoir computing addresses this problem by evolving a fixed dynamical
system and training only a comparatively simple readout
\citep{Jaeger2001,MaassNatschlagerMarkram2002}. Contractive reservoirs are
particularly attractive: contraction makes the effect of initialization
vanish, produces a well-defined causal filter, and gives a direct handle on
fading memory. Quantum reservoir computing instantiates the dynamical system
with a quantum channel and extracts classical features from the resulting
states \citep{FujiiNakajima2017,ChenNurdinYamamoto2020}. This construction can
provide structured nonlinear temporal features, but it also introduces
resource questions that are absent from an abstract reservoir model: how many
qubits encode an input, how many reservoir instances are used, which
observables are read out, and how many state preparations are required to
estimate them.

We study these questions through \textsc{QuaRK}, a quantum-reservoir kernel for
temporal learning. A finite-set Johnson--Lindenstrauss (JL) map compresses each
input vector to the width of a quantum register. A collection of independently
parameterized reset-contractive channels processes a window sequentially.
Expectations of local observables from the resulting states are concatenated
into a classical feature vector, and a Mat\'ern kernel ridge-regression
readout is fitted to these features. The resulting learner is modular: the
projection controls encoded width, contraction controls memory, multiplexing
and the observable family control feature dimension, and RKHS regularization
controls the readout class.

The purpose of the present analysis is not to infer a quantum advantage from
this modular construction. Instead, it identifies what follows from each
module and what does not. In particular, finite-set distance preservation by
JL is not feature injectivity; the ability of a strictly positive-definite
kernel to interpolate distinct features is not a guarantee that the reservoir
produces distinct features; and finite-shot estimation of local observables is
not a device-noise analysis. Keeping these statements separate is necessary
for a falsifiable account of the method.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
Our contributions are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item We formulate a resource-explicit temporal learner built from
    reset-contractive quantum reservoirs and a classical kernel readout.
    Spatial multiplexing is represented as a tuple of independently evolved
    states and a concatenated observable vector, so the model definition does
    not rely on a fictitious tensor-product contraction.

    \item We prove exact trace-norm contraction of every reset channel and
    derive uniqueness of the causal state, loss of initialization dependence,
    and a finite-window feature error that decays as
    \(\sqrt{\sum_r\lambda_r^{2w}}\). A Mat\'ern RKHS stability lemma propagates
    this error to predictions and squared loss.

    \item We characterize finite-sample readout capacity. Conditional on
    pairwise-distinct exact features, strict positive definiteness of the
    Mat\'ern kernel gives a finite-norm interpolant and an explicit upper bound
    on the best training error below the interpolation norm. This result makes
    no claim that the JL projection or quantum reservoir is injective.

    \item We give a one-sided risk bound for a fixed exact featurizer under a
    stationary beta-mixing input--output process. The proof retains the block
    length in the independent-block argument of
    \citet{MohriRostamizadeh2008}, exposes the effective number of blocks, and
    adds the finite-window approximation term. We also state the conditions
    under which geometric mixing yields a
    \(\sqrt{\log N/N}\)-type estimation rate.

    \item We state the local-Pauli classical-shadow requirement separately
    from the learning bound and give a deterministic perturbation guarantee
    for evaluating a fixed RKHS readout on estimated rather than exact
    features.
\end{enumerate}

Together, these results position \textsc{QuaRK} as a resource-aware
quantum-reservoir-based feature map with an analyzable classical readout. They
do not establish computational speedup, statistical superiority over
classical reservoirs, robustness to device noise, or practical deployability
on current hardware.
